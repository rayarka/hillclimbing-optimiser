How this works

Setup. The map is a grid of ground heights. Each climber sees only the slope under its feet and takes a step using one of four update rules: plain gradient descent (SGD), SGD with momentum, AdamW, and a Muon-style rule. The loss is minus the elevation, so going downhill in loss means going uphill on the ground.

AdamW and rotation. (Weight decay is set to zero here, so this is plain Adam under the AdamW name.) AdamW rescales each coordinate separately, so its path changes when you rotate the axes. SGD, Momentum and Muon do not care about rotation.

Muon on a map. Muon orthogonalises the update matrix. Here the parameters are a 2-vector, which is a 1×2 matrix, so the orthogonalised step is just the momentum direction at unit length. That makes it a normalised-gradient method, which is why it sits awkwardly on a 2D map.

Terrain blur. Blurring the map before climbing removes small bumps, so climbers see only the big hills. With “sharpen over the run” the blur fades out as they climb, so they first head for a big hill and then settle on the real top. This idea is called graduated optimization or scale-space annealing. Results are always judged against the real, unblurred terrain.

Arrival. A climber counts as arrived when it ends within about 5 m of the hilltop you would reach by walking steadily uphill from where it stopped. A “flat spot” is a bump under 30 cm of prominence, mostly smoothing and data noise.

Data. Elevation is FABDEM v1.2 (a bare-earth version of Copernicus GLO-30 with buildings and forest removed by machine learning), on its 1″ (~31 m) grid and smoothed with a ~60 m kernel. It is not survey data, so heights can be off by several metres and some small hills are flattened. Bukit Timah Hill reads 162 m here against 163.6 m surveyed, while Bukit Gombak reads about 55 m against 133 m. The tile ends at 104°E, so Pulau Tekong and the far east of Changi are missing. Johor is masked out. Hilltop names are placed by hand and matched to the nearest real peak, so treat them as approximate.
